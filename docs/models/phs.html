PHS Predicted Heat Strain (ISO 7933:2023)

phs-2023ISO 7933:2023 workspaceExploreTime-series

ISO 7933 provides a methodology for calculating the sweat rate and the core temperature the human body develops in response to hot working conditions. The two goals of the analysis are:

  • assessment of the risk of thermal stress to the worker;
  • determination of the maximum permitted exposure times.
PHS is not a comfort model. A PMV of +3 says people are hot; a PHS result says how long before someone is harmed. Do not use a comfort model to assess a hot workplace, or this one to assess an office.

Environmental factors

Input Accepted range Notes
Air temperature 15 to 50 °C The working environment's dry-bulb temperature.
Mean radiant temperature 0 to 60 °C Wide, because radiant heat from a furnace or a hot process dominates many heat-stress cases.
Air speed 0 to 3 m/s Air movement helps until the air is hotter than the skin, after which it does not.
Relative humidity 0 to 100 % The critical input in hot work: it decides whether sweat can evaporate at all.

Personal factors

Input Accepted range Notes
Metabolic rate 0.9 to 3.9 met The work rate. Narrower than the range some published PHS material quotes; this is what the tool enforces.
Clothing insulation 0.1 to 1 clo Work clothing. The model is not validated for heavy protective ensembles.

The simulation also uses body weight and height. The tool supplies standard default values for these, so an ordinary assessment does not require them.

Outputs

Output Meaning
Limiting exposure time How long a worker can remain in these conditions before a limit is reached. The headline number.
Rectal temperature Predicted core temperature at the end of the assessed exposure.
Predicted water loss Cumulative sweat loss over the exposure, in grams.
Limiting criterion Which of the two limits core temperature or water loss was reached first.
The limiting criterion decides the remedy. If core temperature is limiting, the answer is shorter shifts, cooling or reduced workload. If water loss is limiting, the answer is drinking water. Two scenarios can stop at the same time for entirely different reasons.

The two limits

The simulation watches a core temperature of 38 °C and a water loss expressed as a fraction of body mass. The water-loss limit depends on whether workers can drink during the exposure free access to water permits a larger loss than none and a result states which assumption it used.

The eight-hour assessment

On the compliance workspace, PHS is evaluated over a standard eight-hour working exposure. A limiting exposure time of eight hours or more means a full shift can be worked in these conditions; anything less is the safe duration.

PHS in Explore

In Explore, PHS can colour the field by any of three outputs, each with its own default banding:

Output Banded at
Limiting exposure time The eight-hour mark
Rectal temperature after 8 h The core temperature limit
Predicted water loss after 8 h The water-loss limit

This is the quickest way to see how much air speed, or a lighter ensemble, buys you across a range of temperatures.

PHS in Time-series

PHS is the only model that supports the Time-series workspace, and the only one for which a time series means anything: an index like Heat Index has no memory, whereas core temperature and accumulated water loss carry from one minute to the next.

There you build a shift as a sequence of segments each with its own duration, environment and workload and the simulation runs straight through them, the body state at the end of one segment starting the next. This is how you assess a real shift with a rest break, a change of task, or a hotter afternoon.

Edition

The tool implements ISO 7933:2023. The previous documentation described the 2004 edition, so results are not directly comparable with material written against it.

Where to find it

On the ISO 7933:2023 workspace, in Explore, and in Time-series.
